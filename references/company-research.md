# Company Research

Gather real terminology and frameworks so the resume mirrors how the company talks.

## What to research

1. **Job description language.** Words the JD repeats (e.g., "agile", "data-driven", "customer obsessed").
2. **Company values.** Official frameworks, methodologies, or values statements.
3. **Role-specific terminology.** How the company describes this function. "Analytics engineer" vs. "data analyst" vs. "analytics developer."
4. **Recent news or focus.** Is the company investing in a particular area? Migrating a technology stack? This shapes what they are hiring for.

## Using company research

Research shapes **framing and keywords only.** It never becomes invented experience.

**Right way:**
- JD emphasizes "data-driven decision making."
- You have an achievement: "Reduced query execution time from 2.5s to 0.8s."
- You reframe it for the company: "Built real-time dashboards that improved decision velocity for the analytics team."
  - Same achievement, phrased in company language.
  - The core fact (faster queries) is unchanged.

**Wrong way:**
- JD emphasizes "data-driven decision making."
- Your resume says "grew the business by optimizing our data platform."
- You rewrite it to: "Empowered company-wide data-driven culture transformations."
  - This is inventing scope and impact that isn't in your ledger.
  - Do not do this.

## Gathering research

- Read the job description end to end for repeated language.
- Check the company website for values, methodology docs, frameworks.
- Look for company engineering blog posts or case studies.
- Search for recent news (funding, launches, acquisitions).
- Skim LinkedIn profiles of people in this role at this company.

Do not spend more than 15 minutes. The research is for framing, not for becoming an expert.

## The risk

Mirroring company language can feel dishonest. It is not, if you are accurate about facts. You are speaking their dialect, not inventing credentials.

But a resume that is all company keywords and no substance fools the recruiter, not the hiring manager. The hiring manager knows what the role actually requires. Substance wins.

Balance: one-third company language, two-thirds verifiable achievement.
