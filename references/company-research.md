# Company-Specific Knowledge

Two resumes with identical facts can land very differently depending on whether the candidate
sounds like they already speak the employer's language. This step gathers that language and folds
it in, honestly.

## Why it matters

Recruiters and hiring managers pattern-match on their own vocabulary. If an employer calls its
approach "patient-centered care" and the resume says "patient focus", that is fine. But if the
resume shows the candidate understands *why* the organization frames it that way, it reads as
insider fluency instead of keyword matching. The goal is to describe the candidate's REAL work
using the EMPLOYER'S words, never to invent new work.

## What to gather

Search the company plus the role area. Prioritize primary sources.

- **Named frameworks, methods, and programs.** Whatever the employer calls its own way of working.
  Every industry has these: a manufacturer's production system, a hospital's care model, a school
  district's instructional framework, a firm's delivery methodology, a retailer's service standard.
  Use their exact terms.
- **Values and current priorities.** What the company website, newsroom, annual report, and
  leadership emphasize right now. A stated push toward safety, sustainability, cost discipline,
  growth in a segment, or a specific transformation is a scoring signal.
- **Domain and regulatory context.** Industry, customers, compliance environment, accreditation,
  licensing, certification bodies.
- **Tooling and technical vocabulary.** The specific systems, platforms, standards, and equipment
  they name, and how they describe them.
- **The job description itself.** The single richest source of house terminology. Any phrase
  repeated in the JD is a scoring priority. Mirror it wherever it is true.

Good sources: the company's own site, newsroom, and career pages, other postings for the same team,
recent leadership posts, regulatory filings where they exist. Treat third-party summaries as
secondary.

## How to apply it (the honesty boundary)

- **Do** reframe the candidate's true experience in the employer's vocabulary. If a candidate
  reorganized their unit around continuous improvement and the employer runs a named continuous
  improvement system, describe the work using that concept, because they did it.
- **Do** align genuine outcome-oriented experience to an employer's stated philosophy when the two
  are the same idea in different words.
- **Do NOT** claim the candidate delivered the employer's proprietary program, certification, or
  platform if they have not. Speaking a company's language about your own work is fair. Claiming
  their branded methodology is fabrication, and it is trivially caught by anyone who runs it.
- **Do NOT** add a term the candidate cannot explain in an interview. Every borrowed word still has
  to survive the skeptical-interviewer persona.

## The rule for named certifications and programs

Employers frequently name a specific credential in the JD. If the candidate does not hold it, the
resume never implies otherwise. Three honest options, in order of preference:

1. They hold an equivalent credential. Name the real one.
2. They have the underlying experience without the credential. State the experience plainly and
   let the credential gap be a gap.
3. It is in progress. Say "in progress" explicitly, never just the credential name.

## Output of this step

A short list of the employer's real terms, each paired with the candidate's true experience it will
reframe, plus a note of any term with NO honest match. Those become flagged gaps, not keywords to
force.
