# Positioning Lanes

Same facts, different frame, very different outcome. A resume is not a complete record of a career,
it is an argument that the candidate is a particular kind of professional. Choosing which argument
to make is the highest-leverage decision in the build, and holding the line on it is what keeps the
resume honest.

**Analogy.** A restaurant menu is not a lie because it omits dishes the kitchen could technically
cook. It is a claim about what this kitchen is for. But if the menu says "wood-fired" and there is
no wood oven, that is not framing, that is fraud. Framing selects among true things. It never
manufactures one.

## Step 1: Name the lane

Before writing, decide in one sentence what the resume argues the candidate IS for this role, and
write down the boundary it must not cross. Everything in the top third serves that sentence.

Examples of a lane plus its boundary:

- "A supervisor who develops frontline staff. Boundary: does not claim budget ownership."
- "A hands-on practitioner who still does the work daily. Boundary: does not claim management."
- "A generalist operator who can run a small site end to end. Boundary: does not claim deep
  specialist expertise in any one function."
- "A specialist with deep domain depth. Boundary: does not claim breadth across the department."

The boundary matters as much as the lane. Write it down explicitly and check every bullet against
it before delivery.

## Step 2: Hold the line

The pressure to blur a lane is strongest exactly where the JD's requirements are strongest, because
that is where the honest evidence is thinnest. Resist it. These distinctions collapse under one
follow-up question, so blurring them costs the interview, not just the credibility.

The recurring ones, across every industry:

- **Using a tool versus building or owning it.** Running specialized software daily is not
  administering, configuring, or implementing it. Both are valuable. They are different jobs.
- **Internal versus external.** Serving colleagues inside your organization is not serving paying
  customers or clients. Say which one it was.
- **Directing work versus doing it.** Commissioning a report is not writing it. Approving a design
  is not designing it. Supervising a repair is not performing it.
- **Managing projects versus managing people.** Formal authority over hiring, reviews, and pay is a
  different claim from coordinating people who do not report to you. If the candidate has both in
  different roles, frame them separately and never let the stronger one's language bleed onto the
  weaker one.
- **Contributing versus owning.** Being on the team that achieved a result is not having driven it.
  Rescope the bullet to the candidate's actual part.
- **Training versus practice.** A course, degree, or certificate covering a topic is grounding, not
  experience. Never let coursework sit in a skills list as though it were applied work.
- **Currency.** A skill used heavily a decade ago is history, not current proficiency. Frame it by
  what it was and when.

When the JD demands something on the far side of a boundary, that is a flagged gap. Say so in the
delivery summary and let the candidate decide whether to close it for real before applying.

## Step 3: Find the compensating material

Once the lane is set, look for true experience that speaks to the requirement from inside the lane.
This is the craft of the whole skill.

The move is to ask what the requirement is actually FOR, then find the candidate's version of that
purpose. A posting asking for client-facing experience wants someone who can hold up under a hard
question from a stakeholder in real time. A candidate who has defended a plan to a skeptical review
board has done that, internally. Say so in those terms, honestly labeled as internal.

Strong compensating material is specific, owned, and adjacent in substance. Weak compensating
material is a synonym swap. If the only thing connecting the candidate's experience to the
requirement is that both can be described with the same buzzword, it is not compensating material.

## Step 4: Handle the level mismatch

**Applying below the candidate's level.** Overqualification is the main rejection risk. Mirror the
target title honestly in the headline, lead with the parts of their history that map to this job
rather than the biggest-scope items, and let the seniority show through evidence instead of
announcing it. Do not hide years or downgrade real titles. That gets discovered and reads worse
than the mismatch it was meant to solve.

**Applying above the candidate's level.** Lead with the largest scope they honestly own, put the
most senior-flavored responsibilities first inside each role, and make sure the summary uses the
target level's language. Do not inflate titles.

**Changing industries.** Lead with the transferable substance and translate the vocabulary. Name
the new industry's terms only where the candidate genuinely understands them. Expect to spend one
line of the summary explaining the move, because leaving it unexplained invites the recruiter to
invent a worse explanation.

## Positioning by role type

A starting map, not a rulebook. Adapt to the actual posting.

- **Management and supervision.** Formal authority over hiring, performance, and pay is the primary
  claim if it exists. Coaching, training, and scheduling are the primary claim if it does not, and
  they get stated in those words without borrowing management language.
- **Individual contributor and skilled trades.** Lead with what the candidate personally produced,
  with tools, systems, certifications, and standards named at the level of currency they can defend
  today.
- **Client-facing and sales.** Lead with the relationship outcomes they owned, the size and type of
  accounts, and the numbers they personally carried.
- **Operations and delivery.** Lead with scope, throughput, quality, cost, safety, and the specific
  process changes they drove.
- **Analytical and technical.** Lead with the questions they answered and the decisions their work
  changed, then the tools. Tools alone read as a list of nouns.
- **Care, education, and public service.** Lead with population served, outcomes, licensure and
  credentials, and compliance record. Credentials belong high on the page because they are usually
  hard requirements.
- **Career changers and returners.** Lead with the transferable capability and address the gap or
  the pivot plainly in one line rather than leaving it to be inferred.
