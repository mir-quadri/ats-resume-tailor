# Confirm, Then Build

Gaps get resolved by asking, never by guessing. But how you ask determines whether you get a
usable answer or silence. This is the protocol.

**Analogy.** A doctor does not say "tell me about your health." They ask targeted questions with
likely answers attached, because the patient does not know which details are diagnostic. Same
here: the candidate does not know which of their experiences matter to this JD, so open-ended
questions get vague answers and closed questions with good defaults get precise ones.

## Two rounds of questions

**Round one, intake.** Only what the source resume does not already answer and the build cannot
proceed without. Skip anything already on the page. Typically:

- Target title, if the posting's title is not the one to mirror.
- Location and work model, if the posting cares and the resume is unclear.
- Work authorization or licensure, if the posting names a hard requirement.
- Any employment gap visible in the dates, and how the candidate wants it handled.
- Whether any claim on the current resume should be retired. This is the moment to build the
  banned-claims list.

**Round two, gaps.** After the fit assessment, ask only about the specific things the posting
requires that the source resume does not evidence. Patterns in `assets/gap_questions_template.md`.

Never merge the two rounds. Intake questions are answerable immediately. Gap questions only make
sense once the candidate has seen the fit read and decided to proceed.

## How to ask

**Batch the questions.** One round, not a drip of follow-ups. Three questions is a ceiling.

**Make them answerable by choosing, not composing.** Offer two to four short, mutually exclusive
options per question. Include the option that lets the candidate opt out ("leave it off entirely",
"not sure, skip it"), because an honest opt-out is more useful than a guess.

**Recommend a default.** Say which option you would pick and why, in a few words. Candidates
answer faster when they are correcting a proposal rather than generating one from scratch.

**Only ask what changes the output.** If the answer would not alter a single bullet, do not ask.
If the answer is already inferable from the source resume or something they said earlier in the
conversation, use it and do not ask.

**Ask the decision question first.** If the fit assessment was conditional, the first question is
build or skip. The rest are moot if the answer is skip.

## The one-word confirmation rule

This is the rule that prevents the most common failure mode in this whole skill.

When a candidate confirms something briefly, you may use exactly what they confirmed and nothing
more. A "yes" to "have you supervised staff?" licenses the phrase "supervised staff." It does not
license a headcount, an employer, a job title, a date range, or a description of what the
supervision involved. Those are separate facts requiring separate confirmation.

**Write the conservative version and flag the upgrade.** Put the defensible wording in the resume,
then tell the candidate in the delivery summary exactly what detail would strengthen it and invite
them to supply it. This gets the fact into the document immediately while keeping the door open
for the stronger version.

Wrong: the candidate says "yes, I trained new hires" and the resume says "trained 40 new hires
across three onboarding cohorts."

Right: the resume says "trained new hires", and the delivery summary says "if you have a rough
count and can say which employer, that would strengthen this line considerably."

## Handling a fact that arrives mid-build

Candidates remember things after the resume is done. This is normal and often the best material in
the whole document, because the strongest experiences are frequently the ones they stopped
thinking of as work experience.

When a significant new fact arrives:

1. **Add it to the ledger file first**, with a note that it was confirmed in conversation. The
   validator reads that file, so an unrecorded fact will fail the numeric check.
2. **Decide its weight honestly.** If it directly answers a JD requirement you had flagged as a
   gap, it belongs in the top third, not appended to the bottom of an old role.
3. **Place it in every location where it changes the story.** A load-bearing fact usually belongs
   in four places: the summary, the lead "what I bring" bullet, the skills section, and the
   relevant experience entry. A minor fact belongs in one. A fact that directly answers a
   requirement you had flagged as a gap is load-bearing by definition.
4. **Trim to hold the page count.** Adding without cutting pushes a clean two-page resume onto a
   third page. Cut the weakest existing material, not the new material.
5. **Rerun the full verification.** Validator, render, look at it. No exceptions for small edits.
6. **Say plainly what changed and why**, including whether it moved the fit assessment.

## Transcription and dictation

Candidates often dictate. Expect transcription errors in names, acronyms, and numbers. Interpret
intent rather than the literal string, and ask when a garbled token would become a fact in the
document. Never silently "correct" a number, a company name, or a title. Those are exactly the
things that must be right.
