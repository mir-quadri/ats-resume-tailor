# ATS + Reviewer Strategy

This is the playbook for making one resume clear all three gatekeepers. Read it before writing
any bullets.

## Table of contents
1. The three gatekeepers
2. Building the keyword map
3. ATS-safe formatting rules
4. Section order and structure
5. The top third (value proposition)
6. Writing bullets that survive a hiring manager
7. Final self-check

---

## 1. The three gatekeepers

**ATS (the parser).** Software that scans the file for keywords and structure before a human
sees it. It has no judgment. It fails on fancy layouts and rewards exact-match terminology.
Win by: standard section headings, exact JD keywords (with the acronym spelled out once), a
job-title line near the top, and simple single-column formatting.

**Recruiter / HR (the 6-second skim).** A human checking must-haves: right title, enough years,
location, required certs/education, no unexplained gaps. Win by: putting the target title and
years up top, making certs and education easy to find, and mirroring the JD's required-experience
language.

**Hiring manager (the evidence check).** The person who has to believe the candidate can do the
job. They read for owned outcomes, scope, and judgment. Win by: quantified bullets tied to work
the candidate actually owned, recent and relevant experience first, and clear seniority signals.

A resume that pleases only one of these loses. Optimize for all three in the same document.

## 2. Building the keyword map

From the JD, extract and then deliberately place these into the resume where they are TRUE:

- **Target job title** — put the target or a close, honest variant in the headline.
- **Hard requirements** — years of experience, degree, certifications, clearance, domain.
- **Must-have skills and tools** — the specific technologies, methods, and frameworks named.
- **Exact-phrase terminology** — the JD's own words for a concept. If the posting says "case
  management" do not write "client coordination". ATS reward exact matches.
- **Repeated themes** — anything the JD says two or more times is a scoring priority.

For acronyms, include both forms once: "CPA (Certified Public Accountant)". ATS may match either. Do NOT stuff keywords the candidate cannot defend; that is a gap to flag, not a keyword
to place.

## 3. ATS-safe formatting rules

- Single-column layout. No text boxes, no sidebars, no multi-column grids for content the ATS
  must read. Many parsers read columns as scrambled word order.
- Standard section headings, spelled normally: SUMMARY, EXPERIENCE, SKILLS, EDUCATION,
  CERTIFICATIONS. Do not get creative ("My Journey" confuses parsers).
- No critical info in headers/footers. Some ATS ignore them entirely. Keep contact info in the
  document body.
- Standard fonts (Calibri, Arial, Georgia, Times). No graphics, icons, or photos.
- Real bullet characters via the document's list feature, not manually typed symbols.
- .docx format. It parses more reliably than PDF across ATS vendors.
- No em dashes or en dashes. Also an ATS/readability liability. Use periods, commas, or "to".

---

## 4. Section order and structure

Default order for an experienced candidate:

1. **Name + contact line** (city/state, phone, email, LinkedIn). One line, in the body.
2. **Headline** — target title plus a short positioning tag.
3. **Summary** — 3 to 5 lines. Years, domains, the value proposition tuned to this JD.
4. **What I bring to this role** (optional but powerful) — 3 to 5 bullets mapping the candidate's
   strengths directly to the JD's top asks, with proof. This is the fastest way to signal fit.
5. **Core capabilities / skills** — grouped, keyword-dense, all true.
6. **Professional experience** — reverse chronological, most detail on the most recent/relevant
   roles. Older roles compress into an "Earlier career" line.
7. **Education**
8. **Certifications**

Adjust to the candidate's real history; never fabricate to fill a section.

---

## 5. The top third (value proposition)

Recruiters and hiring managers decide in the top third of page one whether to keep reading. That
space must answer, for THIS role: "why is this person an obvious fit?" Load it with the JD's top
two or three priorities, matched to the candidate's strongest true evidence. Generic summaries
("results-driven leader") waste the most valuable real estate on the page.

---

## 6. Writing bullets that survive a hiring manager

- **Own it or drop it.** Every metric attaches to work the candidate personally drove.
- **Outcome, not activity.** "Cut average intake wait from 9 days to 2" beats "responsible for
  intake". Lead with the result the organization cared about.
- **Quantify honestly.** Real numbers (scope, budget, headcount, percentages) build credibility.
  If a number came from a broader effort the candidate didn't own, remove or re-scope it.
- **Mirror JD language** where it is true, so the same bullet scores with the ATS and reads as
  on-target to the human.
- **Front-load the strongest, most relevant bullets** in each role.
- **Plain and short.** No consultant-speak. If a bullet needs a translator, rewrite it.

---

## 7. Final self-check

Before delivery, confirm:
- Target title or honest variant appears in the top third.
- Every JD must-have is either evidenced or flagged to the candidate as a gap.
- Every metric ties to owned work.
- No em/en dashes (run the validator).
- Standard headings, single column, .docx.
- One to two pages, clean render.

---

## 8. Closing the keyword loop

Do not try to hit the keywords on the first draft. Write the honest resume first, then run the
validator, read the missing-term list, and close only what is honestly closeable. Most misses are
wording, not substance: the JD says "graduates" and the resume says "recent grads", or the JD says
"mentoring" and the resume says "coaching". Two passes of honest rewording typically moves coverage
a long way. The last few misses are almost always real gaps, and stopping there is correct. Full
procedure in `build-and-validate.md`.
